Circle of Apollonius

23-step visual proof · Geometry · Analytic Geometry & Circles

Original step-by-step proof (PDF) Construct it in MathLab Split-screen study board Source page & applets

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Steps 2–7

    Look at the upper left area of the screen. When starting a proof, first clearly indicate the assumptions given in the problem on the diagram. Here, the starting condition for finding the trace of a point P, where the ratio of distances from two vertices A and B on the plane is constant m to n (but m is not equal to n, m > 0, n > 0), is depicted in blue symbols.

  2. Steps 8–11

    In triangle PAB, compare sides of the same length and angles of the same size one by one in the figure, centering on the auxiliary line that simultaneously draws the bisector PD (internal point D) of the interior angle ∠APB and the bisector PE (external point E) of the exterior angle.

  3. Step 12

    Check the target shape to the right of the top arrow. This means that we will show why, given the starting assumption on the left, it is always concluded that the locus of point P to the right of the arrow is a circle (circle of Apollonius) whose diameter has its endpoints at points D and E, which intersect line segment AB in m vs. n. Never mix assumptions and conclusions, and now let's build a logical bridge in the proof diagram below!

  4. Steps 14–18

    The interior angle bisector theorem (PA:PB = AD:DB = m:n) and the exterior angle bisector theorem (PA:PB = AE:EB = m:n) hold true, and ∠DPE = 90 degrees (right angle), which is the sum of half of a straight angle of 180 degrees, is always maintained. Accordingly, the principle of point P moving on a circle with the line segment DE as its diameter is completed according to Thales' inscribed angle theorem. What you must remember at this time is the special case that if the distance ratio is m = n (i.e. 1 to 1), it is not a circle but a perpendicular bisector (straight line) of the line segment AB!

  5. Steps 19–21

    Now let's combine the geometric clues we checked earlier into one. From the correspondence relationship between shapes, we can inevitably confirm that the locus of point P, which we wanted to prove, is a circle (Circle of Apollonius) whose diameter has both endpoints at point D and point E, which internally divides the line segment AB into m vs n.

  6. Step 22

    Compare the two areas highlighted in orange and purple. Since the interior angle bisector theorem (PA:PB = AD:DB = m:n) and the exterior angle bisector theorem (PA:PB = AE:EB = m:n) are true, and ∠DPE = 90 degrees (right angle), which is the sum of half of a straight angle of 180 degrees, is always maintained, the principle that point P moves on a circle with the line segment DE as its diameter can be directly confirmed by Thales' inscribed angle theorem. There is. Since the conditions for corresponding sides and angles are exactly met, the two shapes are perfectly congruent (or similar)!

  7. Step 23

    The visual proof is complete! Starting from the starting condition of finding the trace of point P, where the ratio of distances from the two vertices A and B on the plane is constant as m to n (but m is not equal to n, m > 0, n > 0), through an auxiliary line that simultaneously draws the bisector PD (internal point D) of the interior angle ∠ APB and the bisector PE (external point E) of the interior angle ∠ APB in triangle PAB, the trace of point P is divided into line segment AB by m vs. n. It was clearly confirmed that the conclusion holds that it is a circle (Circle of Apollonius) with the inner point D and the outer point E as both end points of the diameter. The key point to remember here is that if the ratio of distances is m = n (i.e. 1 to 1), then the special case is not a circle, but a perpendicular bisector (straight line) of the line segment AB!
