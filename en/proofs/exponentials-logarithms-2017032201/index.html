√(a²b)=|a|√(b) (b＞0)

12-step visual proof · Exponentials, Logs, Trig & Sequences · Exponentials & Logarithms

Original step-by-step proof (PDF) Construct it in MathLab Split-screen study board Source page & applets

Proof at a glance

Starting condition
Given any real number a (positive, zero, or negative) and a positive real number b > 0 inside the radical √(a²b)
What we prove
Regardless of the sign of a, the radical simplifies universally to √(a²b) = |a|√b for all b > 0
Key idea
Since a² ≥ 0 and b > 0, split √(a²b) = √(a²)·√b first, then branch on the sign of a (a ≥ 0 versus a < 0) to match the piecewise definition of |a|
Proof logic
Because the principal square root √(a²) is always non-negative, it equals a when a ≥ 0 and -a when a < 0—which is the exact definition of absolute value |a|
Common misconception
Dropping the absolute value bars and writing √(a²b) = a√b fails whenever a < 0: for a = -3 and b = 2, √((-3)²·2) = √18 = +3√2 = |-3|√2, never -3√2!
What if not?
What if b were negative while a is non-zero? How does introducing the imaginary unit i = √(-1) extend √(a²b) into the complex number system?

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Step 1

    Today we prove the 12-step algebra identity: for b greater than 0, the square root of (a squared times b) equals the absolute value of a times the square root of b. Let us see why those absolute value bars are essential.

  2. Step 2

    In our starting condition, b is strictly positive, whereas a can be any real number—positive, zero, or negative. Keeping both sign possibilities open for a is the key to an error-free proof.

  3. Step 3

    Examine the starting expression: the square root of (a squared times b). Since a squared is always greater than or equal to 0 for any real a, and b is positive, both factors inside the radical are non-negative.

  4. Step 4

    Because both a squared ≥ 0 and b > 0 are non-negative, the product rule for radicals lets us split the square root of (a squared times b) into the square root of (a squared) times the square root of b.

  5. Step 5

    Remember that the radical symbol √(a²) denotes the non-negative square root of a squared. To evaluate it rigorously, we open a piecewise brace and split into two cases: a ≥ 0 and a < 0.

  6. Step 6

    First, when a is greater than or equal to 0, a itself is already non-negative, so the square root of (a squared) equals a directly, giving a times root b.

  7. Step 7

    Second, watch the sign carefully when a < 0! When a is negative, minus a is positive—for instance, if a = -3, then -a = +3. Therefore √(a²) equals -a, and our expression becomes -a times root b.

  8. Step 8

    Look at the two branches together: 'a when a ≥ 0, and -a when a < 0' is the exact piecewise definition of absolute value |a|! Thus both cases unite into a single clean expression: |a| times root b.

  9. Step 9

    Every equality in our chain—from √(a²b) to √(a²)·√b, through the sign cases, to |a|√b—holds without exception for all real a and any positive b > 0.

  10. Step 11

    Reviewing the complete proof on one screen gives a foolproof 1-second habit: whenever you pull a squared variable out of a square root, ALWAYS write |a| first, and only remove the bars after checking the sign of a!

  11. Step 12

    Step 12 (Final Step — Q.E.D.): The visual proof is complete! Splitting √(a²b) into √(a²)·√b for b > 0 and unifying the a ≥ 0 and a < 0 cases via absolute value proved √(a²b) = |a|√b universally. And if b < 0 in higher algebra, writing b = -|b| extends the result cleanly to |a|√(|b|)·i! Check out the original PDF and interactive quizzes in the description.
