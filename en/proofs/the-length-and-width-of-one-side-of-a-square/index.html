The Length and Width of one Side of a Square

20-step visual proof · Geometry · Euclidean Plane Geometry

Original step-by-step proof (PDF) Construct it in MathLab Split-screen study board Source page & applets

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Steps 7–11

    Now, apply the idea of finding the area of a square by subtracting the area of the corner right triangles from a large square surrounded by four right triangles and a small square on graph paper, which is the key to the proof. After this conversion, the structure of an expression that seemed complex can be clearly solved using basic operations that we already know.

  2. Steps 12–15

    One side of a square with an area of 1, 4, or 9 is the natural numbers 1, 2, or 3, but one side of a square with an area of 2 or 5 cannot be expressed as a rational number, so the equation is derived without any gaps according to the principle of accurately expressing the length of a side by introducing a new symbolic root (radical). What to pay special attention to at this stage is that when the area is doubled, the length of a side is not doubled, but the length of a side is doubled as the root!

  3. Steps 16–19

    If we combine the formulas organized in the previous step into one, we can finally naturally conclude that the length of one side of the square whose area we were aiming for is a (a is a positive number) is expressed as a positive number that becomes a when squared, that is, the symbolic root a (positive square root)!

  4. Step 20

    The visual proof is complete! Starting from the starting condition of observing the relationship between the area S of a square and the length of one side It was clearly confirmed that the conclusion holds that a is a positive number, that is, it is represented by the symbolic root a (positive square root). The key point to remember here is that doubling the area does not mean doubling the length of a side, but don't be confused by the fact that the length of a side becomes twice the root! Finally, ask a deeper question. If I take the root of 2, the length of one side of a square with an area of 2, and trace it with a compass on a vertical line, will the irrational number perfectly correspond to a point on the vertical line?
